Michaelis–Menten

[S]′ = −Vmax[S] / (KM + [S])

How fast does an enzyme use up its substrate? The equation is separable, so its solutions satisfy KM ln([S]/[S]₀) + [S] − [S]₀ = −Vmaxt exactly. Where it comes from

An enzyme E binds substrate S into a complex C, which releases product P:

E + S ⇌ C → E + P  (rates k₁, k₋₁, k₂)

Mass action gives [S]′ = −k₁[E][S] + k₋₁[C] and [C]′ = k₁[E][S] − (k₋₁ + k₂)[C], with free enzyme [E] = E₀ − [C].

If the complex settles quickly (quasi-steady state), [C]′ ≈ 0, so [C] ≈ E₀[S]/(KM + [S]) and [S]′ = −k₂[C]: the equation above.

What the terms mean

  • [S]′: the rate of change of substrate (negative), so −[S]′ is the rate it is consumed. At t = 0 that rate is V₀ in plots 2 and 3.
  • Vmax = k₂E₀: top speed, with every enzyme busy. k₂ (kcat, the turnover number) is the substrate converted per enzyme per unit time at saturation.
  • KM = (k₋₁ + k₂)/k₁: the [S] giving half of Vmax. Lower means the enzyme works well at lower substrate. It equals the dissociation constant k₋₁/k₁ only when k₂ ≪ k₋₁, so it is not strictly a measure of binding affinity.
  • [S]/(KM + [S]): fraction of enzyme occupied. About [S]/KM when [S] ≪ KM (exponential decay), about 1 when [S] ≫ KM (linear decay).

When the reduced model applies

  • Quasi-steady state: after a brief initial burst, [C] adjusts fast enough to track its steady value as [S] slowly falls.
  • Little enzyme: E₀ ≪ [S]₀ + KM. Tick “Full model” to see it fail.
  • Simple chemistry: one substrate, no inhibitors or cooperativity, product formation effectively irreversible (early in the reaction).
  • Well mixed, at fixed temperature and pH.

The full-model overlay sets k₁ = 1.

How biochemists use it

  • Characterizing enzymes: fit V₀ against [S] (plot 2) for Vmax and KM, then compare kcat = Vmax/E₀ and kcat/KM across enzymes and mutants. Plot 3 is the Lineweaver–Burk linearization; direct curve fitting is now preferred.
  • Drug discovery: inhibitors shift the apparent KM and/or Vmax (competitive ones raise KM and leave Vmax unchanged), showing how a drug acts. Statins, competitive inhibitors of HMG-CoA reductase, are one example.
  • Drug metabolism: liver enzymes saturate, so doses can raise blood levels disproportionately (phenytoin, ethanol).
  • Assays and industry: enzyme-activity tests, and choosing enzymes for brewing, detergents, and biofuels.

How are the curves computed?

The red curves in plot 1 come from the exact implicit solution above, solved for [S] at each time with Newton's method (no numerical ODE solver). The blue full-model curves have no closed form, so they use a fixed-step fourth-order Runge–Kutta method with a step small enough for the fast binding rates.

The blue dots in plots 2 and 3 are what an experiment would measure from the full model: the peak product-formation rate k₂[C](t), reached right after the initial binding burst. They lie on the reduced curve when E₀ is small and fall below it as E₀ grows.
